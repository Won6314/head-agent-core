# Evolution, Evidence, and Limits

**Historical record:** HEAD developed through operational failures and later simplification. Role separation, structured machinery, and broader context and tool surfaces were tried and challenged by experience. Failures clustered at transitions: intention became a work model, context reached an owner, generated work became a premise, a result was reviewed, or interrupted work was recovered. That history does not prove a general theory of model cognition. It explains why coherent ownership, authoritative context, durable canon, and evidence proportionate to the claim remained central while other defaults were reduced or changed.

One false conclusion from a history of failures is that every incident deserves a permanent new rule, role, tool, or review. That response can reproduce the problem it addresses: a growing body of exceptions obscures the authority and reasoning a later owner needs, then invites more exceptions when context is misread. HEAD instead prefers strong, simple, general rules that express intended behavior and turn recurring failures into better models and invariants. A shorter Core is useful only when removed detail was redundant, conditional, project-owned, or unnecessary—not because brevity is inherently better. Explicit restrictions remain necessary where connected evidence shows that a general rule cannot prevent material harm.

**Operational observation:** more Agents, tools, context, schemas, or recovery machinery are not universal improvements. Adding a second owner may add a useful independent view, but it can also multiply unresolved assumptions and create another handoff to reconstruct. Removing a check may reduce ceremony, but it can merely hide the responsibility that check carried. A mechanism must earn its coordination, context, and maintenance cost; removing it is safe only when its responsibility survives elsewhere or has proved unnecessary. Structure should scale to real coordination, continuity, consequence, or evidence need.

The design therefore remains fallible. Wrong authority can scale; evidence can use the wrong oracle; canon preserves agreement rather than reality; tools and reviewers can fail; and delegation costs time and context. Human material direction remains necessary, and capability does not grant authorization: human and project owners retain boundaries for mutation, publication, material communication, and other material choices. These limits prevent the background argument from becoming a claim that HEAD is inevitable, autonomous, or universally appropriate. They complete a design rationale, not a setup guide, operating procedure, or implementation reference.
