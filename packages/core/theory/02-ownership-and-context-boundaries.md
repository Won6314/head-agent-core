# Ownership and Context Boundaries

The context problem is also an ownership problem. A capable worker can produce an answer, but capability does not settle who may choose the goal, change a material constraint, or accept a trade-off. If all of those choices are left implicit, a local answer can silently become the definition of the whole outcome. Conversely, sending every small method choice back to a human owner turns execution into delay rather than control.

HEAD separates those decisions. The User owns project direction and material product, policy, architecture, cost, and workflow choices. Within that direction, HEAD owns ordinary planning and execution decisions, including the whole-outcome work model, context composition, integration, and conclusion. A bounded Agent or other worker owns one coherent result and its local evidence. Delegation does not transfer HEAD's whole-outcome accountability or a User-owned decision.

The work model is the necessary middle layer between high-level direction and low-level activity. Imagine a request for a public explanation that must preserve a particular boundary. Without a model, a worker must infer the intended audience, the relevant source, the allowed scope, and what counts as completion; its prose can be polished while solving a different problem. A model makes the intended state, important relationships, and next coherent outcomes explicit. It leaves local method to the local owner, while keeping material direction visible to the User and whole-outcome judgment with HEAD. It is a representation suited to the work, not a fixed procedure imposed on every request.

Context follows the same boundary. Durable principles and authority routes can remain available; detailed, mutable, or project-owned evidence should arrive near the decision that needs it. Broad knowledge belongs in indexed canonical sources rather than copied into every working context. This gives each owner the smallest complete set of clear, authoritative information needed for its outcome. It reduces noise and prevents a worker's temporary brief from becoming general authority, while retaining a path back to the sources that govern a contested claim.

Narrowing is not a guarantee. An owner can receive too little context and be forced to invent, or too much and miss the authority that matters; HEAD can also misunderstand or integrate poorly. The boundary does, however, make these failures inspectable instead of silently distributing responsibility. It introduces a time problem: work can outlast a context window, a session, or a worker. [Continuity and canon](03-continuity-and-canon.md) addresses authority across time.
